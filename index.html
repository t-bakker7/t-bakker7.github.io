5V to 1.8V synchronous buck converter

Waterloo Aerial Robotics Group, electrical team

A compact 1 A power supply board built around the TI TPS62A01. I chose every part and checked it against the numbers: a 1 µH inductor that handles a 1.24 A peak current, a 4.7 µF input capacitor for about 20 mV of input ripple, a 22 µF output capacitor, and a 200 kΩ / 100 kΩ divider that sets the 1.8 V output.

The board has test connectors on the input and output and an LED that shows when 5 V is present.

UNITE: a self-driving lab for cosmetic formulation

Acceleration Consortium, University of Toronto · Fall 2026 to present

An LLM proposes a recipe. A robotic system makes it, tests it, and sends the results back. The loop repeats until the LLM converges on a formulation that works. I'm building the robotic system from the ground up.

Dispenser

A commercial scale we reverse-engineered so Python can read and command it. Custom stands and ingredient stations that the arm moves into place. A PID controller handles materials from low-viscosity liquids to solids.

Transfer

A UR5 six-axis arm, also reverse-engineered for control from Python. It moves the ingredient stations into position for dispensing.

Heater

A hotplate with a reverse-engineered control interface, so the orchestrator can heat a batch as one step of the recipe.

Mixer

A custom two-axis base lifts a beaker up to a reverse-engineered homogenizer. A peristaltic-pump cleaning system washes the hardware between runs.

The hardest part was software integration. SiLA (Standard in Lab Automation) is the communication layer between the modules and the orchestrator, and every device needed its own connector, so we wrote the drivers. All of it is Python under Git, with a lot of 3D printing, stepper motors, and servos on the hardware side.

More builds and studies

Low-noise, long-range PIR sensor conditioner

Self-directed project for MTE 220, built on a TI reference design

A two-stage band-pass amplifier from TI's Analog Engineer's Circuit Cookbook. It turns the millivolt signal from a PIR motion sensor into one a window comparator or ADC can use, with 90 dB of gain between 0.7 and 10 Hz on a single 5 V supply. I taught myself how the active filters work before they came up in class, then built the circuit in LTspice and ran AC and transient analyses.

At 2 Hz, a 10 mV input came out of the first stage as 2.5 V ± 1.77 V, matching the predicted 45 dB, and the second stage clipped to the 0 V and 5 V rails as expected. The AC sweep showed less gain than the design called for, which I suspect is the simulator's op-amp model not matching TI's part.

LTspice · TLV9062 · Active band-pass filters · AC and transient analysis
